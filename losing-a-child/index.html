Grief

Losing a child.

There are no words that can hold the pain of losing a child. Whether they were a baby, a child, a teenager or an adult, it is a loss that seems to turn the order of life upside down. Perhaps nothing anyone says makes sense right now. Here, there is room for your pain, with no rush and no expectations.

You are still their parent

Your child is still part of your story. And you are still their mum or dad.

Saying their name, talking about them and keeping their memory present are ways of loving. Everyone finds their own way to carry that bond.

When the day asks too much

After losing a child, even small things can take an enormous effort. Getting up, eating, answering a message. Some days you manage; on others, everything weighs heavy. You don’t have to expect the same of yourself every day.

If a smile comes, a good conversation or a moment of lightness, you don’t need to feel guilty. Those moments can exist too, alongside the longing and the love for your child.

“Could I have protected them?”

The “what ifs” can come back again and again. Guilt can appear even when there was no way to foresee or prevent what happened.

You may look back and wish you had done something differently. But what seems clear today may have been impossible to know at the time. You deserve the same understanding you would offer someone you love.

The questions that hurt

“How many children do you have?” can be a hard question to hear.

You can include the child who died, answer another way, or choose not to talk about it in that moment. There is no right answer. You don’t have to explain your story to everyone.

Together, each in your own way

Two people can love the same child and grieve in very different ways. One may need to talk; the other, silence. One may look for company; the other, a little time alone.

These differences don’t measure love or longing.

Brothers and sisters are living this loss too. Sometimes they stay quiet because they don’t know how to talk about it, or because they are afraid of adding to their parents’ pain. They also need space to feel and to be heard.

What can help, a little

  • Accept help with meals, shopping or housework. If it’s hard to say what you need, someone you trust can help organise it.
  • Take time alone if you need it, and look for company when you feel like it. Your needs can change from one day to the next.
  • Keep your child’s room and belongings for as long as it feels right. There is no deadline to decide.
  • Choose how you want to spend birthdays and other important dates. You can create a tribute, be with someone, or have a quieter day.
  • Talking with other parents who have lost a child can bring a feeling of being understood, without having to explain everything.

Where to find support

You can find support groups especially for mothers and fathers who have lost a son or a daughter. Being with people who have lived a loss like yours can help you feel understood, without having to explain everything.

Questions

Will I ever feel joy again?

It is possible to feel joy again, even if that seems far away right now. For some people, it comes back in small moments and learns to live alongside the longing. Smiling doesn’t erase your love for your child.

Is it normal to find it hard to be around other families?

Yes. Seeing other families can bring up sadness, anger, longing or a sense of injustice. You can say no to invitations and choose what you can face right now, without justifying yourself more than you want to.

What can I say to a friend who has lost a child?

You can say: “I’m so sorry. I’m here with you.” You don’t need to find an explanation or try to ease the pain with a phrase. Listen, offer practical help, and keep being there as time goes on. If they want to talk about their child, make room for that conversation.
